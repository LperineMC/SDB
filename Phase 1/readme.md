Dataset Provenance and Characteristics: The data for the primary corpus was sourced from a database created by the Legacy of Slavery project at the Maryland State Archives (https://slavery.msa.maryland.gov/).  With assistance of staff and volunteers, the LoS project took digitized images of domestic traffic ads and transcribed information in those images into data tables. The resulting database was made accessible via a web interface.  Researchers at the University of Maryland’s Advanced Information Collaboratory (AIC) crawled the website and scraped the data using a “browser automation” technique (reference the Jupyter notebook on AIC website).  The “crawled” data yielded a file of 2836 rows. Based on prior experience with LoS collections and their sociocultural context, additional data cleaning and validation was conducted by AIC researchers to produce a “clean”, computationally-ready dataset for analysis in several research studies (reference AIC).

Primary Corpus. The primary corpus contained in Notebook 1 consists of a fixed sample of fifty (50) newspaper advertisements documenting the sale and hiring of enslaved people in Maryland between 1824 and 1864. The records originate from the Maryland State Archives Legacy of Slavery project and represent a subset of a larger database of datafied records documenting the commercial trafficking of enslaved individuals within the state. This particular corpus provides an informative stress-test for SDB as a methodological artifact, due to conditions of extreme commodification, violence, and structural silences present at the origin of the records. 

The primary corpus is treated as a fixed evidentiary dataset for purposes of this experiment. The same fifty records are used without substitution in all three experimental conditions/ 

The original “crawled” dataset, rather than the computationally-ready version produced by AIC researchers, is used to constitute the primary corpus in this study. The “crawled” data simulates conditions where data has not yet undergone preparation for computational treatment. This also allows an informal comparison of the experiment results and actual choices were made by AIC researchers as they prepared.

Notebook 2 materials were curated by the researcher and instantiated within a dedicated NotebookLM notebook, separate from the primary corpus in Notebook 1. 

The second knowledge repository (Notebook 2) used in this study is a contextual knowledge notebook implemented in NotebookLM (reference and link). Notebook 2 contains eighty-four (84) curated sources with information that contextualizes the domestic slave trade and the commoditization of enslaved people in Maryland. The Notebook 2 materials constitute an independent evidentiary resource intended to contextualize the origins of information encoded in the Notebook 1 primary corpus. Notebook 2 curated sources fall into six broad categories:
•	Archival Guides and Historical Datasets The sources feature extensive guides from the Maryland State Archives, detailing the massive "Legacy of Slavery in Maryland" database. This includes the specific "Chattels" dataset, which catalogs thousands of bills of sale, appraisals, and property records to trace the commercialized dehumanization of the slave trade and the specific trafficking routes utilized by slave dealers. Other highlighted records include certificates of freedom, manumission deeds, and runaway dockets that provide the raw demographic data of the era.
•	The Economics and Accounting of Commoditization .Several monographs and economic analyses dissect the stark financial mechanics of the domestic slave trade, which funneled hundreds of thousands of individuals from the Upper South to the Deep South. They detail how major auction houses and traders in hubs like Baltimore (featuring notorious dealers like Austin Woolfolk and Hope H. Slatter) and Richmond utilized complex accounting to value human life. The texts explore how traders used fractional hands (e.g., "quarter hands"), standardized commodity grading, and physical metrics like height and weight to reduce human beings to fungible, interchangeable assets on "prices current" circulars.
•	Legal and Regulatory Frameworks. A robust collection of transcribed Maryland statutes (1642–1860) outlines the rigid legal scaffolding that enforced the slave economy. These include laws mandating the public sheriff's sale of unclaimed suspected runaways to cover jail fees, regulations penalizing those who harbored or transported fugitives on railroads and steamboats, and codes restricting the immigration and rights of the free Black population.
•	Regional Agricultural Shifts and Labor Markets The sources extensively document Maryland’s "middle ground" transition from a tobacco-dependent economy to mixed-grain and wheat agriculture. This shift diminished the economic viability of year-round enslaved labor in northern and western Maryland, prompting landowners to rely heavily on seasonal hiring markets. Consequently, planters commoditized their "surplus" enslaved laborers by selling them to the booming cotton and sugar economies of the Deep South, driving the massive interstate slave trade. 
•	Resistance, Flight, and Social Dynamics A series of essays, documentary transcripts, and museum exhibit summaries emphasize the agency, resilience, and trauma of enslaved individuals. These sources instruct researchers to read runaway advertisements as testaments to rebellion and the agony of family separation. They also explore the mechanics of the Underground Railroad, the reliance on maritime escape networks (the "Black Jacks" of the Chesapeake), and how the state's uniquely large free Black population interacted with those still in bondage.
•	Archaeological and Anthropometric Studies To fill the silences left by written records, the collection includes material culture analyses, such as archaeological surveys of slave quarters and burial sites across Maryland plantations (e.g., Hampton, L'Hermitage, and numerous Prince George's County estates). Furthermore, the sources feature anthropometric histories that utilize slave manifests and certificates of freedom to quantitatively measure the physical stature and nutritional deprivation of enslaved African Americans.
